🧬 What to Focus on for Bioinformatics Essays
✅ Lead With
• Your MATA biomedical coursework — show you already think like a bioinformatician
• GMU AI & Medical Informatics research — real research experience at 17 is rare
• The intersection of biology + technology — that's the field's identity
• Specific questions that excite you (genomics? drug discovery? AI in diagnostics?)
• Your CNA experience — understanding patient data from both sides
🚫 Avoid
• Saying you "always loved computers and biology" — too generic
• Framing bioinformatics as a fallback from medicine — own the pivot
• Vague goals like "I want to help find cures" — be specific about what problem you want to solve
• Listing skills without showing how you've applied them
• Ignoring the research component — 80% of admitted students have research experience
💡 Core Essay Angle for Bioinformatics: "I started in patient care — as a lifeguard and CNA — and realized the most impactful problems in medicine live in the data. Bioinformatics is where I can work at that intersection." That pivot from caring for individual patients to working on systems-level biology is a compelling, specific story.

📐 Paragraph-by-Paragraph Structure
1
The Hook — Open on the Pool Deck
~80 words
What it does
Drop the reader into a specific moment on the pool deck. Don't explain it yet — just put them there. The best essays open in the middle of a scene, not with "I have always wanted to be a doctor."
Write about one specific moment as a lifeguard — a moment of tension, a close call, or a quiet moment where the weight of responsibility hit you. What did you see? What were you thinking? What did you do?
Sensory, present tense, specific. The reader should feel the heat, hear the noise, see what Rishi sees when he scans the water.
2
Zoom Out — The Bigger Pattern
~100 words
What it does
Step back and show the pattern started before the pool deck. Boy Scouts, swimming, the lifeguard certification — same instinct, different chapters.
Touch briefly on Boy Scouts and swimming — not as achievements but as earlier chapters of the same story. What did they teach you? What do they share with lifeguarding?
Don't list. Connect. One or two sentences each, then land back on the pool deck.
3
The Turn — From Safe to Healed
~120 words
The most important paragraph
This is the pivot. Something changed the question from "how do I keep this person safe?" to "how do I understand what's happening inside them?" This is where lifeguarding meets medicine.
Was there a moment where you wished you knew more — what was happening inside someone's body, not just what to do on the outside? Or a MATA moment that clicked with something from the pool? That intersection is the heart of this essay.
This should feel like a light turning on. The shift from "I keep people safe" to "I want to understand why bodies fail and how to fix them" is the emotional core of the whole piece.
4
MATA & HOSA — Where Safety Became Science
~120 words
What it does
Bring in the biomedical program — not as a résumé item but as the place where the curiosity sparked by lifeguarding found a home. What specific thing from MATA changed how he sees things?
Pick one specific thing from MATA — a concept, a lab, a topic — that excited you and connect it back to your lifeguard experience. "When I studied cardiac physiology in MATA, I thought about the time..." is more powerful than "I have learned a lot about the human body."
Specific always beats general. Name an actual topic. Mention HOSA briefly as the community that connected learning to a larger healthcare world.
5
The Close — What He's Ready For
~80 words
What it does
End with forward momentum, not a summary. The reader already knows who Rishi is. Close with where he's going and why it feels inevitable — not chosen.
What does medicine mean to you now — after all of this? What kind of doctor do you want to be, and why does your path make that feel inevitable? Circle back to the opening image if you can.
Don't say "I want to be a doctor to help people." Say something specific. The kid who scanned that pool every shift is the same person walking into medical school.

📐 Bioinformatics Essay Structure
1
The Hook — A Moment at the Bedside
~80 words
What it does
Open with a specific moment from CNA training or lifeguarding — but this time the observation is different. Not "how do I help this person?" but "what is actually happening inside this person that I can't see?" The data question, sparked by the bedside moment.
Was there a patient during CNA clinicals who made you think about their biology differently — a condition, a vital sign, a reaction that made you want to understand the data underneath? Or a lifeguard moment where you wished you could see inside the person you were watching?
Sensory and specific — same opening technique as the BS/MD essay, but the question at the end of the scene is different. Not "how do I save them?" but "what does the data say?"
2
The Computational Thread — Robotics to MATA to GMU
~100 words
What it does
Show that the interest in applying computation to biological problems didn't start with bioinformatics — it started with robotics in middle school, grew through MATA's biomedical curriculum, and crystallized at GMU's AI & Medical Informatics program.
How did your interest in technology + biology build over time? Robotics was about machines solving problems. MATA showed you the biology. GMU's AI program showed you they could work together. Trace that thread in 2–3 sentences without listing accomplishments.
This paragraph earns the pivot. The reader needs to believe this interest is genuine and deep — not a course correction from medicine but a natural evolution of the same curiosity.
3
The GMU Research — The Most Important Paragraph
~130 words
The centerpiece — most important paragraph
This is where Rishi has something most 17-year-olds don't: actual research experience presented at a real conference. Don't summarize the research — describe the moment when the computational analysis revealed something about the biological data that surprised him. That's the bioinformatics "aha."
What specific finding or moment from the GMU AI research genuinely surprised you or changed how you thought about something? What did the data show you that you couldn't have seen any other way? What question did the research leave unanswered — the one you want to pursue in college?
Be specific. Name a tool, a dataset, a finding. "When our model identified a correlation between X and Y, I realized..." is powerful. "I did research on AI in medicine" is not. The July 17 conference presentation is worth mentioning explicitly.
4
Why Bioinformatics — Not Medicine
~110 words
Address the pivot directly — this is what every admissions reader is thinking
Don't avoid the question — own it. The strongest answer isn't "I changed my mind" but "I realized I can have more impact here." A bioinformatician working on genomic data touches millions of patients. A physician touches hundreds. That's not a retreat from caring — it's a different form of it at a different scale.
Why bioinformatics specifically — and not medicine? What can you do in this field that you couldn't do as a physician? What problem do you want to work on — genomics, drug discovery, AI diagnostics, population health data? Be specific about the scale and type of impact you're drawn to.
Do NOT frame this as a fallback. Do NOT say "I still might go to med school." Own the choice. Admissions readers respect clarity and conviction — hedging reads as uncertainty.
5
The Close — The Question He's Chasing
~80 words
What it does
End with forward momentum — not a summary of what he's done, but the specific question or problem he's pursuing next. Circle back to the opening patient moment if possible. Show that the bedside observation in paragraph 1 is still driving him.
What specific research question or problem do you want to work on in college? What will you do in your first year — which lab, which professor, which dataset? End with the question the opening patient moment posed — and how bioinformatics is how you'll answer it.
Forward momentum always beats backward summary. The last sentence should leave the reader believing this student is already doing the work — not planning to someday.

🧠 Core Strategies — Reading & Writing Focus
📖 Reading & Writing — What to Fix
→Vocabulary in context — practice picking the right word for the sentence's tone, not just meaning
→Transitions — however, therefore, furthermore questions. Learn the 6 types cold
→Central idea & purpose — always ask "what is the author trying to do in this paragraph?"
→Evidence & data questions — practice reading graphs and matching claims to data correctly
→Grammar rules — subject-verb agreement, comma splices, apostrophes. 10 rules, drill them daily
📐 Math — Hold & Improve
→Linear equations & systems — highest frequency topic, must be automatic
→Quadratics — factoring, vertex form, discriminant. 3–4 questions per test
→Ratios & proportions — word problems are predictable, learn the setups
→Never leave a math question blank — no penalty for guessing, eliminate and pick
→Calculator section discipline — don't over-rely on calculator, it slows you down
🛠️ Daily Habits That Move the Score
→Read quality nonfiction daily — 20 min of dense reading (NYT, Atlantic, science articles) trains the RW section faster than drills alone
→Review every wrong answer — don't just mark it wrong. Understand exactly why the right answer is right
→Timed practice only — untimed practice builds false confidence. Always use a timer
→Khan Academy daily — linked to College Board, adapts to his weaknesses automatically
📅 Test Day Strategy
→August 22 is the date — treat every full practice test as a dress rehearsal
→Sleep Aug 20–21 — the two nights before matter more than the night of. No cramming
→Flag and move — never spend more than 90 seconds on one question. Flag, move, return
→October backup — if Aug 22 comes in below 1500, October SAT still covers Nov 1 deadlines. The pressure is real but not final
